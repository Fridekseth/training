From 19. to the 21. November I swapped the studio for the sea, boarding a ship in the west coast of Norway together with PhD fellow Jørgen Dalen at OICL. We spent two days aboard, observing day to day routines, conducting interviews and testing and co-creating designs with the crew.

The ship we visited has wavefoils installed, so we got to see the technology in use. This was valuable to both my project on learning and the parallel OpenZero project on decision support, as both are exploring how bridge interfaces in OpenBridge can help crew learn and make the most of new technology, using Wavefoil as a case.

## What we did

### Observations

Through silently observing routines, we got a feel of the rhythm of the work. When it's hectic, when there is spare time, where there is friction and what runs smoothly. Details like how shifts are done, how the crew collaborate with each other, and how they physically move around between different equipment on the ship was very interesting to witness first hand.

### Interviews

Conversations with the crew let us into their world. What is important to them, what motivates them and what holds them back. They also shared the technical side of their operations, like how different equipment work and how it is used in different scenarios. It was really valuable to have time to get to know the users, and talk to them while in the work setting.

### User testing

We brought both the digital prototypes and printed concept sketches with us, that we tested on the crew. The feedback was generally positive, and the crew was eager to give feedback. They were impressed by the visual style of OpenBridge, that they said was clean and easy to read. The areas of improvement were mainly technical details, like what data is most important to show and enhance. When seeing the interfaces in their right environment and in the hands of the users that could eventually use them in their everyday life, it became very clear what worked and what needed more polishing.

### Co-creation sessions

After having shown the crew our sketches, we went back to the drawing board to quickly sketch up new iterations based on their input. This allowed us to design the interface there and then, together with the users. Details like what data is most important for them to see, how they would like to view it, and other technical things was very useful to design together with the experts.

## Findings

What struck me most was how much collaboration a single operation involves. The Wavefoil technology doesn't just live on a screen on the bridge. It's operated and maintained across the whole vessel, from the chief engineer in the engine room to the captain on the bridge. So far I have viewed the systems as quite isolated to the screens on the bridge, but now I've seen how they are connected to the whole vessel and crew.

## Back in the studio

My experiences in the field, became a strong reminder of why it matters to visit the environments we design for. Meeting our users eye to eye shifted my perspective on the project, and now I'm taking these insights back to the studio for a final polish.

I think my collaboration pattern has a lot of potential, when it comes to dealing with shift based workplaces and communication on the vessel. I also think the second screen idea could be useful to bring the interfaces out of the bridge. We've seen that everything is already interconnected on the ship, so why should the interfaces be fixed to the bridge?

Thank you to the captain and crew for sharing their experience, and to the shipping company and Wavefoil for the collaboration.
