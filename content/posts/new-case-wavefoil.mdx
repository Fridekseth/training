The next step in the process is to take my [first draft of the training
concept](/blog/visuelt-sprak-for-opplaering), and adjust it to different use cases, with
Wavefoil being the first one. Before I start sketching, I have talked to users to gain a
deeper understanding of the system and environment the training needs to address.

![Line drawing of a ship seen from the bow, with its foils extending below the waterline](/posts/new-case-wavefoil/vessel-with-foils.webp)

## Why Wavefoil

I chose to work with Wavefoil as a second case, as it has an interactive interface that
introduces a new aspect to my training concept. My first case, the Orkla interface, is for
monitoring, while on the Wavefoil screen the crew controls the foils, so the training has to
let users try the system without affecting it. It is also a case where I have access to
users, allowing me to involve them throughout the process.

## About the technology

Wavefoil is a company that makes bow foils for ships. The foils reduce the motion of the
vessel in waves, which makes the ride calmer and can save fuel. The system is a good example of
secondary equipment on a ship bridge, that is typically not prioritized when onboarding new
crew. This makes it all the more relevant to provide a built in solution for easy teaching
of the system.

## Talking to users

I talked to one employee at Wavefoil, to learn more about the technology and need from
their point of view.

I learned that the training is mostly verbal today. When a vessel gets the foils, the
employee often joins the crew on a trip to demonstrate the foils. In the beginning the crew
are typically sceptical and afraid to make mistakes, so a big part of this initial
introduction is to make them feel safe with the system. Over time, he told me users often
build their own theories about what works best. Some of these theories aren't necessarily
based on real data or tests, but he is careful not to dismiss them as he does not want to
discourage the engagement. I also learned that the screen on the bridge is small, with
physical buttons next to it, so the training needs to work in a compressed view.

I also talked to two captains on boats that has the foils installed. Their experiences
lined up well with what I had already learned: Neither of them had received any real
training on the system, and both have learned it by using it. They are also typically the
lone operator the foils when sailing, so they don't have someone to ask if they are unsure.
The foils are secondary to the work on the bridge, and both described how they rely on what
they see and feel in the boat, more than on the numbers on the screen. One of them told me
that in operation, all his attention need to go to the manoeuvring of the vessel, so he can
only look on the Wavefoil screen for than a couple seconds at a time. The thing they worry
about most is forgetting to retract the foils, for example when approaching a harbour where
it can damage the equipment. Neither gets any feedback from the system on what the foils
actually save them, and both ask for the same thing in the interface: keep it simple.

## Prototyping

Based on these conversations, I will start the work on training prototypes for the
Wavefoil interface. It seems like my idea to let users test an application's functionality
in a safe environment can be very promising in this case. I would also like to test out the
idea of adding comments, so captains can have an easy way to share experiences despite not
working together. Finally, the screen on the bridge is small, and the equipment differs
between vessels, so the training needs to work in a compressed version of the interface as
well as in the larger one.
